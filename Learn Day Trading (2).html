Read this first: Day trading is one of the riskiest ways to trade. Most people who try it lose money — and with margin, you can lose more than you put in. This guide teaches the skill in the safest order: learn, practice with fake money, then start tiny. It is education, not financial advice.
A beginner's curriculum · 8 phases

Learn day trading, step by step — without paying tuition to the market.

Day trading means opening and closing positions within the same day. The craft is learnable, but only in the right order: market basics first, charts second, one strategy third, and risk management before — and above — everything. Follow the phases below in sequence.

Most beginners lose

FINRA requires brokers to warn that day trading “can be extremely risky” and is not for anyone with limited resources, investment experience, or a low risk tolerance. Treat losses early on as near-certain — the goal of Phases 1–6 is to make them happen with fake money.

Costs compound quietly

Spreads, slippage, commissions, platform and data fees, and short-term taxes shave every trade. A strategy that looks profitable on a chart can lose money after costs — which is why you track them from your very first practice trade.

The rules changed in 2026

FINRA replaced the old “pattern day trader” rule and its $25,000 minimum with risk-based intraday margin standards, effective June 4, 2026 (brokers may phase in through Oct 20, 2027). Your broker now watches whether your equity covers your positions during the day. Details in Phase 2.

The path

Eight phases, in order. Don't skip.

Each phase ends with a hands-on exercise and a milestone. You are ready for the next phase only when you can honestly say you've hit the milestone. Tap a phase to open it.

Learn this

  • What a share is: a slice of ownership in a company. Its price moves because buyers and sellers disagree, minute by minute, about what that slice is worth.
  • Long vs. short: going long = buy now, sell later, profit if price rises. Shorting = borrow shares, sell now, buy back later, profit if price falls. Short losses are theoretically unlimited — leave shorting alone until Phase 8.
  • Bid, ask, and spread: the bid is what buyers offer, the ask is what sellers want. The gap (spread) is a cost you pay on every trade, whether you see it or not.
  • The four core order types: market order (execute now, at whatever price), limit order (execute only at your price or better), stop order (triggers a market order when price hits a level), and stop-limit (triggers a limit order). Beginners should default to limit orders.
  • Sessions: the main US stock session runs 9:30 a.m.–4:00 p.m. ET. Pre-market and after-hours trading has thin volume and wide spreads — avoid it as a beginner.

Do this

Exercise (30 min): Pick one famous, heavily traded stock (a large company you know). Watch its live quote for 15 minutes without trading anything. Write down the bid, the ask, and how much both moved. Then explain — out loud, in one sentence each — what a market order and a limit order would have done if you'd placed them.
Milestone: You can define long, short, bid, ask, spread, and all four order types without looking them up — and say why a market order in a fast-moving stock can fill far from the price you saw.

Learn this

  • Cash account: you trade only with money you've deposited and that has settled (US stocks settle next business day, T+1). No borrowing, no owing more than you have. The right starting point.
  • Margin account: your broker lends you money to trade bigger. Losses are amplified, the broker charges interest, and if your equity falls too far you get a margin call — the broker can sell your positions without asking.
  • The 2026 rule change: FINRA's new intraday margin standards (effective June 4, 2026; brokers may transition through October 20, 2027) replaced the pattern day trader rule. There is no longer a $25,000 minimum or a day-trade count. Instead, your broker monitors your equity during the day against your open positions. If it falls short, you have an “intraday margin deficit” you must fix as promptly as possible; if it's still open after five business days, your account can be restricted for up to 90 days. Some brokers still show the old rules during the transition — ask yours which applies to you.
  • Choosing a broker: regulated in your country, a good paper-trading simulator, fast and reliable order entry, transparent fees, and real-time data. Fancy charting matters less than reliability.
  • Taxes (US): day-trading profits are generally short-term capital gains, taxed at your ordinary income rate. The wash-sale rule can disallow losses if you rebuy the same stock within 30 days — frequent traders hit this constantly. Keep records from day one and talk to a tax professional before going live.

Do this

Exercise (1 hour): Open a paper-trading (simulated) account with a regulated broker — most major brokers offer one free. Find, in writing: its commission and fees, whether your account would be cash or margin, and which margin rules it currently applies. Save all three answers in a notes file; you'll need them in Phase 8.
Milestone: You can explain the difference between a cash and margin account, what an intraday margin deficit is, and why you'll start in a cash account even though margin “lets you do more.”

Learn this

  • Candlesticks: each candle shows one period's open, high, low, and close. The body is open→close; the wicks are the extremes. Long wicks mean rejection of that price; long bodies mean conviction.
  • Timeframes: day traders typically plan on the daily chart, find setups on the 5-minute chart, and time entries on the 1-minute chart. One timeframe alone lies to you regularly.
  • Trend: uptrend = higher highs and higher lows; downtrend = lower highs and lower lows; otherwise it's a range. “Trade with the trend” is cliché because fighting it is the most expensive habit beginners have.
  • Support & resistance: price levels where buying (support) or selling (resistance) previously overwhelmed the other side. Prior day's high/low, the opening range, and round numbers are the levels day traders watch most.
  • Volume: how many shares changed hands. Moves on high volume are more trustworthy than moves on thin volume. VWAP (volume-weighted average price) is the one indicator most day traders keep: price above VWAP = buyers in control today, below = sellers.
  • Keep it sparse: VWAP plus one moving average (e.g., 9-period EMA) is plenty. A screen full of indicators creates confidence, not accuracy.

Do this

Exercise (daily, 2 weeks): Use the interactive chart lab below, then graduate to real charts. Each evening, take one stock's 5-minute chart from that day and mark: the trend, two support/resistance levels, and where price sat versus VWAP at noon. No trading — just reading. Save a screenshot in your journal folder.
Milestone: Given any 5-minute chart, within 60 seconds you can state the trend, the two nearest key levels, and whether buyers or sellers controlled the day — and be right most of the time when you check the outcome.

Learn this

  • The 1% rule: risk no more than 1% of your account on any single trade (many beginners use 0.25–0.5%). On a $5,000 account, one trade may lose at most $50. This single rule is why some traders survive their learning curve and others don't.
  • Position size is derived, not chosen: shares = (account × risk %) ÷ (entry price − stop price). The calculator below does this math. If the stop is far away, you trade fewer shares — you never move the stop to fit a bigger size.
  • The stop-loss is decided before entry: it's placed at the price that proves your idea wrong (e.g., below the support you bought at), entered as a real order or a hard mental rule you never break. “I'll get out if it goes bad” is not a stop.
  • Reward-to-risk: only take trades where the realistic target is at least twice the distance to your stop (2:1). At 2:1, you can be wrong 60% of the time and still break even before costs.
  • Daily loss limit: a hard number — commonly 2–3% of the account — at which you close the platform and stop for the day. Most blow-ups are one bad hour, not one bad trade.
  • Drawdown math: lose 10% and you need +11% to recover; lose 50% and you need +100%. Protecting capital is worth more than any single winning trade.
The one warning that matters: never trade on margin, never average down into a losing day trade, and never risk rent, tuition, or borrowed money. If a loss would hurt your life, the position is too big — full stop.

Do this

Exercise (45 min): Run five scenarios through the position-size calculator below: vary the account size, entry, and stop. Then write your personal risk rules on one page — per-trade risk %, daily loss limit, max trades per day — and date it. This page becomes the constitution your Phase 7 journal is graded against.
Milestone: Given any entry and stop price, you can compute your share size by hand in under 30 seconds, and your written risk rules exist as a document, not an intention.

Learn this

  • A strategy is a complete rule set: what you trade, when you enter, where your stop is, where you exit, and when you don't trade at all. “Buy when it looks strong” is not a strategy.
  • Why only one: every strategy loses in some market conditions. If you run three half-learned strategies, you can't tell whether a losing streak is the strategy, the market, or you. One strategy, deeply tested, gives you data you can trust.
  • Backtest by hand: scroll a chart back 20 trading days, hide the future, and “trade” your rules bar by bar on paper. Log every signal — including the ones you'd have skipped. Twenty honest samples beat two hundred cherry-picked ones.
  • Expectancy: (win rate × average win) − (loss rate × average loss). Your backtest's job is to find whether this number is positive after costs. If it isn't on paper, it won't be with real money.

Do this

Exercise (several sessions): Choose one strategy from the cards below and write its full rule set on one page. Then hand-backtest it over 20 past trading days on a single stock or ETF, logging at least 20 trades with entry, stop, target, and result in your journal spreadsheet.
Milestone: You have a one-page written strategy, 20+ backtested trades logged, and a computed expectancy. If the expectancy is negative, you revise the rules or pick a different strategy — you do not proceed on hope.

Learn this

  • The four emotional traps: FOMO (chasing a move that's already gone), revenge trading (immediately trading bigger to “win it back”), overtrading (trading from boredom), and moving your stop (refusing to be wrong). Every experienced trader has paid for all four.
  • Process goals beat money goals: “Follow my rules on 3 trades” is controllable. “Make $200 today” is not — and chasing it rewrites your rules in real time.
  • The pre-market routine (see the full timeline below): check the day's economic calendar and earnings, build a short watchlist of 2–4 stocks, mark key levels, and write your plan before the open.
  • The first 30 minutes are the wildest: spreads are wide and moves are violent at the open. Many beginners do better trading 10:00 a.m.–12:00 p.m. ET and the last hour, and sitting out the open entirely while learning.
  • End the day deliberately: close all positions (that's what day trading means), log every trade, and review against your rules — not against the money.

Do this

Exercise (one week): Follow the routine timeline below for five days with no trading at all — build the watchlist, mark the levels, write the plan, then watch whether your Phase 5 strategy's setups appeared. This rehearses the discipline without the adrenaline.
Milestone: You have a written daily routine, you've run it five times, and you can name your personal #1 emotional trap and the specific rule that guards against it.

Learn this

  • Simulate honestly: use a paper account the size you actually plan to fund — not $100,000 of pretend money. Use realistic position sizes from your Phase 4 rules, and count the simulator's fills as optimistic (real fills are usually worse).
  • Journal every trade with the template below: setup, entry, stop, target, size, result, whether you followed your rules, and a screenshot. The journal — not the profit — is the product of this phase.
  • Grade the process: your key stats are rule-adherence % (aim: 90%+), expectancy per trade, max drawdown, and whether your daily loss limit was ever breached. Profit with broken rules is luck and doesn't count.
  • Review weekly: group your trades by setup and by mistake. Most beginners discover one leak — often trading outside their plan, or cutting winners and riding losers — that costs more than everything else combined.

Do this

Exercise (4–6 weeks): Paper trade your single strategy, minimum 40 trades, following your written risk rules exactly. Download or copy the journal template below and fill it in for every trade, the same day. Do the weekly review every Friday.
Milestone: 40+ journaled paper trades, 90%+ rule adherence, expectancy at or above breakeven after costs, and no daily-limit breaches in the final two weeks. Miss any one of these and the fix is more paper trading — not real money.

Learn this

  • Real money changes the psychology, not the math: expect your paper-trading discipline to wobble. Counter it with size so small the money genuinely doesn't matter — many coaches suggest starting with share counts in the single digits.
  • Fund only true risk capital: money you can lose entirely without changing your life, in a cash account, separate from your savings and your long-term investments.
  • Halve your paper risk: if you risked 1% per trade on paper, risk 0.25–0.5% live for the first month. Your only job in month one is executing the plan under real emotion.
  • Scale on evidence, not feelings: increase size by a small step (e.g., +25%) only after 20 live trades that match your paper stats. Two consecutive daily-limit breaches = back to paper for two weeks. No exceptions, no negotiations with yourself mid-session.
  • Keep the day job math honest: track your all-in costs and your hourly result for the first quarter. Most people conclude day trading works best for them as a small, disciplined side activity — or not at all. Both are successful outcomes of this course; blowing up an account is not.

Do this

Exercise (before funding): Complete the readiness checklist at the bottom of this page, out loud, item by item. If any box would be a lie, that's your next task — go back to the phase it belongs to.
Milestone: You've traded live at minimum size for 20 trades, journaled all of them, stayed within every risk rule, and your live expectancy roughly matches your paper results. Only now does scaling up enter the conversation.

Phase 5 · Choose one

Four starter strategies

Each is a complete, testable rule set — not a guarantee. Pick the one whose rhythm fits your schedule, write it on one page, and backtest it before paper trading. All four are long-only: no shorting while you learn.

Opening Range BreakoutACTIVE · FIRST HOUR

Trade the break of the first 15 minutes' high or low, in the direction of the break.

  • Setup: a liquid stock moving on news or earnings, with a clear 9:30–9:45 a.m. range.
  • Entry: limit order just beyond the range high (for longs) once a 5-min candle closes outside it.
  • Stop: the midpoint of the opening range. Target: 2× the stop distance.
  • Skip if: the range is wider than ~1.5% of price (too risky) or volume is fading.

VWAP PullbackSTEADIER · MIDDAY

In an uptrend, buy the first orderly pullback to VWAP instead of chasing strength.

  • Setup: price trending above VWAP all morning, higher highs and higher lows.
  • Entry: price pulls back to VWAP and a 5-min candle closes back above it.
  • Stop: just below the pullback's low. Target: the day's high, then 2:1 minimum.
  • Skip if: price slices through VWAP on heavy volume — the trend may be done.

Support BouncePATIENT · LEVELS

Buy at a pre-marked support level where buyers defended price before.

  • Setup: price approaches a level from your pre-market plan — prior day's low, a round number, a multi-test floor.
  • Entry: after a rejection wick or a small higher low at the level — not while it's still falling.
  • Stop: below the level (if it breaks, your idea is wrong). Target: the next resistance level.
  • Skip if: the whole market is selling off hard — support levels fail in cascades.

Momentum Gap & GoADVANCED · FAST

Stocks that gap up on real news often trend early. Exciting — and the easiest to blow up on. Attempt only after the other strategies feel boring.

  • Setup: gap up ≥4% on a genuine catalyst, pre-market volume heavy, price under ~$50 for manageable size.
  • Entry: first 1- or 5-min pullback that holds above the gap level / VWAP.
  • Stop: below the pullback low, no exceptions. Target: scale out at 2:1; these fade fast.
  • Skip if: no identifiable news behind the gap — unexplained gaps trap beginners.
Phase 6 · The trading day

A day trader's day, hour by hour

Times are US Eastern for the New York session. Notice how little of the day is actual trading — most of the job is preparation and review.

8:00 – 9:00 AM

Prepare

Check the economic calendar (Fed speakers, inflation or jobs data can wreck any setup), earnings releases, and overnight market direction. Build a watchlist of 2–4 stocks with a reason for each.

9:00 – 9:30 AM

Plan

Mark yesterday's high/low and key levels on each watchlist chart. Write today's plan: which strategy, what would trigger an entry, your max loss for the day. Then stop touching it.

9:30 – 10:00 AM

Watch (beginners: hands off)

The open is the most volatile, widest-spread half hour of the day. While learning, observe and note the opening range. Let experienced traders have it.

10:00 AM – 12:00 PM

Prime trading window

Trends are established, spreads are normal, volume is strong. Execute only setups that match your written plan. Stop the moment you hit your daily loss limit or your planned number of trades.

12:00 – 2:00 PM

Midday lull

Volume dries up and prices chop sideways — a classic overtrading trap. Step away, eat, review your morning trades. Most beginners should simply not trade this window.

3:00 – 4:00 PM

Final hour

Volume returns as institutions position into the close. One planned setup at most. Everything must be closed by 4:00 p.m. — holding overnight turns a day trade into a different, unplanned risk.

4:00 – 4:30 PM

Journal & review

Log every trade with a screenshot while it's fresh. Grade rule-following, not profit. Note tomorrow's levels. Then close the platform — the day is done.

Phases 7–8 · Put it to work

Your journal template & go-live checklist

The journal is where learning actually happens. Use these exact fields for every paper and live trade.

Trade journal template

Date: Symbol: Strategy / setup: Why this trade (one sentence): Entry price: Stop-loss: Target: Shares (from the calculator): Risk $ and % of account: Exit price: Result $ and R multiple: Followed my rules? (Y/N — and if N, which rule): Emotion before / during (1–5): Screenshot saved? (Y/N) Lesson:

Readiness checklist — all true, or keep practicing

  • I can explain every term in the glossary without looking.
  • My strategy is written on one page, with entry, stop, target, and skip conditions.
  • My risk rules are written: per-trade %, daily loss limit, max trades per day.
  • I hand-backtested 20+ trades and my expectancy is not negative.
  • I paper traded 40+ trades with 90%+ rule adherence.
  • I never breached my daily loss limit in my final two paper weeks.
  • My account is a cash account funded only with money I can afford to lose entirely.
  • I know my broker's fees and which margin rules apply to my account.
  • I have a plan for taxes and records, or a tax professional to ask.
  • My first live size is small enough that a full losing day changes nothing in my life.
